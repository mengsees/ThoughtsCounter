Thought Counter

Practice observing your thoughts instead of being carried away by them. Tap each time you catch one, during meditation or any other time, and see the calm stretches between your thoughts.

How it works

Settle in

You get 5 seconds after pressing Start. The clock begins once you are ready.

On a phone

Tap the big button, or anywhere on the screen, for each thought. Tap Undo if you slip.

On a computer

Press Space or left-click anywhere. Backspace or a right-click undoes the last one.

Finish

It ends by itself at the set length. Or tap End (twice), or press Esc twice. Cancel session discards it.

Why it helps

Observing is one of the most valuable skills you can train. Each time you catch a thought and tap, you practice stepping back and watching it pass, instead of being carried away by it. That steady practice is how we train our mind.

Use it during meditation, or at any other time: on a walk, at your desk, while you wait in line. Choose Open-ended or set a custom length, and tap whenever you catch a thought.

Why count your thoughts?

  • Observing is the practice. Each tap marks the moment you realize your attention has drifted from your anchor. That moment of recognition is the skill you are training.
  • It makes the mind visible. Thoughts feel constant and shapeless. Counting turns them into separate events you can see, compare and come back to.
  • It keeps you out of the story. A quick tap is lighter than following a thought or judging yourself for having it.
  • It gives you something to look at afterwards. Patterns that are hard to feel in the moment show up clearly on the chart.

What you can observe

  • Settling time. How long did it take for the first thought to come?
  • Calm stretches. How long was your longest calm stretch, and what came right before it?
  • Bursts or an even flow. Thoughts often arrive in clusters. A cluster can follow a trigger such as a sound, an itch or a worry.
  • Across sessions. Use the notes (sleep, time of day, stress, caffeine) and the Past sessions list to see what changes the pattern.

A higher count is not worse. As you get better at observing, you may catch more thoughts, so look at the gaps rather than treating the number as a score. This is a self-report tool for curiosity, not a medical measurement.
